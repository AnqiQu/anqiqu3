OpenAI announced a few days ago that one of its models had produced a solution to the Navier-Stokes problem (one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems). I read this on my phone, in bed, and then went back to sleep. Thinking back, that was probably the most surreal part. Reading news like that should have landed harder than it did, but it didn't, because some version of it now comes out roughly every other week.

Much conversation has revolved around what AI can do but I'm more interested in what happens to us when it can. Many mathematicians have already come out to say that AI is ending the field as they know it and there is a general aura of depression amongst my mathematician friends (though that may just be because they are mathematicians). Maths is hardly the only frontier. AI systems are now edging into problems that humans have spent decades, sometimes centuries, trying to solve. What happens to the people working in fields that demand technical depth, creativity, and passion - like drug discovery, scientific research, engineering (and even, per Anthropic's latest threat report, missile development, scamming, and mass surveillance) - when AI can do it all? If more and more things that used to be difficult become easy, what remains valuable and what will we actually care about?

Chess could be an interesting precedent. Computers have been better than every human at chess for nearly thirty years, and people have not stopped playing. If anything, more people play now than ever. Nobody sits down at a board thinking, "Well, what's the point when Stockfish would do this better?" Because the point, it turns out, was never simply to produce the best sequence of moves. There is something we value intrinsically about the playing itself. Although few would argue that chess is actively pushing the frontier of humanity, perhaps this is a useful thought to hold onto as we head into a world where machines can do pretty much anything (and much better than we can, too).

I'm reminded of an argument I had with a friend recently about using AI to make art. I was saying that I don't really understand why an artist would want to use AI in their work at all. His perspective was that sometimes an artist has a vision that they can't bring to life on their own and AI closes that gap. I said that just means they're a bad artist. Skill issue. He said that more art is strictly better for society. I said I didn't think AI art counts. It went on like that for a while. Underneath the bickering, though, there was a real disagreement, and it took me a while to work out what it actually was. Let me try to do it properly here.

When we say a piece of art is valuable, what is the thing we value about it? Is it the image itself? The idea behind it? The skill it took to make? The time somebody spent on it? Or simply the fact that another human being made it?

Imagine that AI eventually lets us generate essentially unlimited numbers of technically excellent paintings, songs, films, novels, and games. It's tempting to conclude that art has become abundant, but that's not quite right. Images have become abundant. Songs have become abundant. Text has become abundant. And relative to all of that, some other things get scarcer: human attention, human time, provenance, good taste, cultural consensus, experiences shared with other humans, etc. My guess is that is probably where economic and social value migrates.

This isn't a new idea and we already see versions of it everywhere. Industrial manufacturing didn't eliminate expensive clothing but rather helped turn handmade clothing into a luxury good. Quartz movements didn't destroy watches but rather helped turn mechanical watches into objects that people value partially because they are unnecessarily difficult to make. The inefficiency becomes part of the product. Therefore if AI makes competent aesthetic production almost free, complete handmade art probably becomes more valuable. It's for this reason that I just don't think it's GTO for artists to want to incorporate AI into their art because those who do will be the ones who benefit least from it.

(One caveat before this turns into an argument that anything difficult is therefore valuable: scarcity on its own does not create value. The thing still has to be desirable. My terrible stick-figure drawings did not become worth $50,000 the day Midjourney launched.)

Now back to my argument with my friend, I think it ultimately came down to two different theories of art. His is something like: "Art is the realisation of an idea". An artist has something they want to express, their technical limitations get in the way, and AI expands the space of ideas they can realise. In that sense, AI is just another tool: a camera, Photoshop, a synthesizer, CGI, a particularly nice paintbrush... 

Mine is closer to: "Art is partly constituted by the act of making it". The technical limitation isn't an annoying obstacle sitting between the artist and their vision; wrestling with it is part of what it means to practise an art. A pianist who can imagine a piece they cannot play doesn't get to say they performed it no matter how well they can describe what it ought to sound like. We keep composing and performing as separate achievements for a reason.

My friend's other argument deserves a fairer hearing than I gave it at the time. It's the classic abundance argument: if more people can express themselves to the degree they envision, then more art exists, and society gains more cultural value. Simple, and in some sense true. But there is another side to the ledger. Human attention is finite. Suppose the world currently produces a hundred thousand things you might enjoy, and tomorrow it produces a trillion. Your capacity to enjoy art has not increased by a factor of a million. It probably hasn't increased much at all. You still have the same number of hours in a day. Connecting infinite supply to a finite pool of attention doesn't create value in proportion to the amount produced. (Mostly it creates a sorting problem. This, I guess, is why Silicon Valley spent a good chunk of this summer talking about "taste" as though they had just discovered the word, and I have thoughts about that, but they're for another essay.)

Follow the thread of finite human attention far enough and you eventually arrive at what I think is the most important scarcity of all: a human life. There are still only twenty-four hours in anyone's day, and only so many years (unless Bryan Johnson somehow proves the rest of us wrong). This means that anything containing a substantial amount of another person's time may become particularly meaningful in a world where almost everything else is easy to produce. Someone cooking dinner for their friends, a handwritten letter, a professor spending an hour talking to one student, a painting somebody worked on for a year. Perhaps the luxury goods of an abundant future won't be technologically sophisticated at all. Perhaps they'll be things that prove another human being chose to spend some of their finite life on you.

~~~

So somewhere around here, I realised that I wasn't really writing about art anymore. I was writing about time.

I genuinely believe that, if we play our cards right, an age of extraordinary abundance is coming. But that world isn't quite here yet, and I feel the weight of that pending abundance in a way that is difficult to describe.

There is so much happening right now that it is very easy to feel that every spare hour of my life ought to be spent working: learning more, making something, pushing things forward, contributing in some tiny way to bringing that future about. But I also keep thinking about my future self. I imagine being eighty years old and asking what I would give to be twenty again. The answer will probably be: Anything. And then I remember, I am in my early twenties! I've somehow been handed, for free, the exact thing that I imagine my future self would trade almost anything to get back. This feels like an odd kind of moral obligation. If I truly believe that my time is the scarcest thing I possess, then surely I shouldn't spend all of it preparing for some future in which everything else is abundant.

There are things available to me now that won't be available later. Being young. Having very few responsibilities. Sitting up until 3 a.m. talking to friends. Wandering around a city with nowhere particularly important to be. Falling in love. Making stupid decisions that I will probably find funny eventually. Having enough uncertainty ahead of me that my life can still go in almost any direction. These things are scarce too.

Of course, the answer can't be to spend all my time enjoying being young and none of it building towards the future. I know myself well enough to know that wouldn't make me happy either. But the answer can't be zero. This is my own Pareto frontier: how much of the present am I willing to trade for the future, and how much of the future am I willing to trade for the present? I'm still figuring out where on that frontier I want to sit (and unfortunately UChicago econ didn't help with this). Maybe abundance doesn't abolish scarcity but rather just reveals which scarcities mattered all along. If machines can produce the painting, write the song, solve the problem and manufacture the object, then perhaps the valuable question becomes less "What can be made?" and more "What did someone choose to spend their life making?" 

Surely the same is true of a life itself.
