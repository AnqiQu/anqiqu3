---
title: My Month Up Here
subtitle: Musings on The Muse.
date: 1 September 2026
---

When the symptoms of my (AI) psychosis became hard to ignore, I decided a change of air would do me good. Somewhere elevated. Somewhere with a view. So off I went to San Francisco.

I sublet a room in a community house called The Muse, which sits at the top of a steep hill in NoPa, high enough that an electric Lime scooter would sometimes struggle to make it home. I told everyone I was staying for a month. It wasn't until the third subletter arrived that I understood where I actually was.

For anyone who hasn't read The Magic Mountain: a young man called Hans Castorp goes up to a sanatorium in the Swiss Alps to visit his cousin for three weeks. He ends up staying seven years. The place is called the Berghof and it is less of a hospital than a very comfortable hotel where everyone happens to be ill, and where the doctors, at every examination, find a reason to recommend another six months. Time stops working properly up there. The patients call themselves "we up here" and everyone else "down below" and after a while they can't imagine why anyone would want to go down.

I couldn't help but feel that I had somehow made it to the Berghof while staying at The Muse. Nearly everyone I met there was on some kind of cure. (At least) two were recovering from breakups of the kind that required a change of city. One was a recovering finance bro who was retraining as a philosopher. Another was recovering from their twenties and had moved halfway around the world to become a different person before they turned thirty. Nobody had a fever (except for the few days after we all got sick from doing hot yoga together), but everyone had some condition, and everyone was quietly fond of it, the way Mann's patients compare temperatures over lunch.

I asked a lot of people how they ended up in San Francisco and the stories all had the same shape. They came for two weeks, or for the summer, or to see a friend. Then they extended. Then they moved. Then they joined something, or started something, and now it's been four years and they're the ones asking newcomers how long they're staying, with a small, knowing smile. I understood the smile within a week. The city is an escape from real life and, at the same time, the most intellectually alive place I have ever been. Those two facts are supposed to be in tension. Up here they aren't.

My days began to take on some semblance of structure, which in a sanatorium is called a regimen. Wake up. Scavenge breakfast from whatever the last grocery order produced. Take morning calls. Scavenge lunch. Scoot to a public library for an afternoon work session. Come home for dinner. Spend the evening wrapped in blankets on the squish (a pillow pit that occupies most of the living room floor and is very hard to leave once entered). Mann gives his patient two tutors, Settembrini and Naphta, who argue for hundreds of pages about reason, faith, progress, and death, with total conviction and no effect on anything. Evenings on the squish were like that. A typical night moved, without anyone marking the transition, from whether Hegel should be treated as an authority on consciousness (a real argument, with sides, that got heated), to whether one of us should text an ex (a real argument, with sides, that got more heated), to whether we needed more oat milk in the next grocery order. All three were conducted with the same intensity. 

Side note paragraph:
I loved the public library. It's the best people-watching in the city because it's the one place where nobody is performing. Nobody LARPs as a founder at the public library. There was a man who I think was schizophrenic who stood up every twenty minutes or so, shouted a Bible verse or something that sounded like Nietzsche then sat back down. A man wearing a Canada Goose parka, latex (!) gloves, sunglasses, cap, and N95  mask, reading the Chronicle in a room that was seventy degrees. A really fat man who sat across from me and played on a Game Boy for four hours straight without once looking up (which is a longer period of sustained focus than I saw from anyone in a coworking space all month).

Now here is the question I couldn't stop asking. I'll put it plainly, because the sanatorium frame is really just a way of asking it gently. Am I actually getting closer to the thing? Or have I just become very good at inhabiting an environment organized around people who are trying to get there? Talking about startups can feel like building a startup. Talking about research can feel like doing research. Meeting impressive people can feel like becoming more impressive. Being surrounded by ambition can feel like accomplishment. None of these feelings are lies, exactly, but the only person who can read the chart is me, and I'm the patient. Mann's answer, as far as I can tell, is that Hans really does get an education up there and really does waste seven years, and both are true, and you cannot tell which is which from the inside. I find that more honest than either of the usual verdicts on this city.

As for how my treatment has been going. The doctors (me) have reviewed the case and are recommending another six months. I told everyone I was staying for a month. I don't know yet what my thunderbolt will be, the thing that comes up the mountain and takes me down whether I'm finished or not, because nobody at the Berghof leaves on account of getting better. I'm choosing to believe this is a difference between me and them. I'm aware that's what all of them believed too.
