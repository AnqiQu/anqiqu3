Okay so I know that UChicago economists proposing solutions for problems in developing countries do not have a good historical track record, but hear me out... 

At this moment, it is harder than usual for everyone, and particularly young people, to find work. We've all heard the facetious jokes about entry-level jobs requiring ten years of experience, prompting the quip that you need a job to get a job. Unfortunately for millions of young people, it's not particularly funny at all. Imagine being 23, having done everything society told you to do, and discovering that there is simply no place for you in the economy. You went to school, worked hard, and now spend your days sending applications into the void. How do you continue to be optimistic about your future?

I know that this is a problem across the world right now, but for this piece I will focus on South Africa. Stating the obvious here but South Africa has quite a serious youth unemployment problem. Nearly HALF (!) of the young people participating in the labour market can't find work. The consequences extend far beyond the obvious economic costs. A young person who wants to contribute but cannot find an opportunity represents skills that aren't being developed, businesses that aren't being built, and economic activity that never happens. Unemployment also makes it harder to achieve independence, establish a household, support family members, and generally build a life. It can perpetuate dependence across generations and entrench existing inequalities.

I could go on, but I think I'm beating a dead horse here. We all know youth unemployment is bad. What I want to talk about is a simple idea that I think could help.

Something I've been reflecting on, being a young person myself, is what a privilege it is to be able to fail. People love to tell young people to take risks. Start a company! Try something new! Don't worry about making mistakes! If your stock portfolio is not down at least 10% today then you're playing it too safe! (Okay that last one may be just from my gambler friends, but still.) However, taking risks is expensive and the consequences of failure are not fairly distributed. I consider myself very fortunate in that if I were to take a significant career risk and fail, the floor of my possible outcomes would probably still be fairly comfortable. For many others, the floor is much lower. Taking a few months to pursue an uncertain and often unpaid professional opportunity might mean being unable to afford rent, food, or failing to contribute to a household that depends on their income. The rational decision may be to avoid taking the risk at all.

We often associate risk-taking with ambition and risk aversion with a lack of it, but two equally ambitious people can make completely different decisions simply because they face different consequences if things don't pan out. The ability to experiment with one's life is, in many ways, a luxury good. This has led me to wonder whether one of the best ways to unlock ambition is simply to make failure less expensive such that trying something new becomes a rational decision. 

Not to mention that opportunity compounds. A summer internship leads to a professional reference, and perhaps to a graduate job, which leads to a mentor, which leads to a promotion, which opens the door to the next opportunity, etc etc. And the opposite can compound too. A period of unemployment makes it harder to get your first job and the longer you've been unemployed, the more employers wonder why nobody else has hired you. This is one of the uncomfortable things about meritocracy. We like to think that professional success is purely a process of identifying and rewarding talent but in practice, employers can only observe demonstrated talent, and to demonstrate talent, someone must first give you an opportunity to demonstrate it. Before I get sidetracked, the point I want to make is that since opportunity compounds, I think that we should be particularly interested in subsidising the first opportunity because that maximises the leverage of its long-term effects.

When I was a student at the University of Chicago there was this thing called the Jeff Metcalf Internship Program. This program blew my mind. It allowed students who had found unpaid internships (or other substantive professional experiences) to apply for funding from the university so that they could pursue these opportunities comfortably. Opportunities that would otherwise have been inaccessible to many students. From very early on, students were encouraged to go out into the world and find something interesting to do. They could approach a small startup, a research lab, an NGO, or an organisation that had never considered hiring an intern before. The Metcalf funding arrangement took a lot of pressure off both sides by lowering the financial barrier for the student while making it easier for organisations to say yes.

What I found particularly remarkable was the freedom it afforded us to experiment. You could spend a summer in a field you knew little about, discover you loved it, or discover you hated it, and either outcome would be a win. (I worked for a summer at a hedge fund, discovered that listening to earnings calls made me depressed, and that was the end of a career in buy-side finance for me.) And concurrently, students could build a portfolio of experiences, develop relationships with employers, and acquire a much clearer sense of the careers they wanted to pursue by the time they graduate.

Of course, UChicago students already enjoy a rather extraordinary set of advantages, and I don't mean to suggest that transplanting a university program into the entirety of South Africa would solve all our problems. But that got me thinking... If this kind of opportunity can be so valuable for people who are already relatively privileged, how transformative might it be for someone who has never had access to a professional environment at all?

### So here's my proposal: 
What if we create a fund that enables young South Africans to find and pursue their own first professional experiences?

The premise is simple. If you're a young person who identifies a meaningful opportunity to learn and work, but cannot afford to pursue it, the fund helps cover the cost of making it happen. 

It's a fund that follows the young person rather than the employer. This is deliberately different from a centralised government internship program that allocates young people to placements. For two reasons. Firstly, it gives people agency: you choose where to go, which means you're invested in making it work. Secondly, it opens doors into industries that don't usually offer internships at all. I imagine that plenty of small businesses, workshops, studios, and start-ups would happily take on an eager young person if it didn't cost them anything.

Now just to quickly throw in another UChicago economics reference here. Friedrich Hayek once argued that the knowledge needed to run an economy well isn't sitting in a central office but rather scattered across millions of civilians, each of whom knows their own corner of the world. A central agency trying to place a million young people would have to know which bakery in the neighbourhood needs an extra pair of hands, which architecture studio would take a junior, and which young person would thrive in each. It can't. But the young person, together with their family and community, often can. 

The most obvious benefit is that young people gain real work experience and learn more about different careers, which overall improves labour-market matching. There are also many less tangible benefits. One of these is access to networks: Professional relationships are a form of capital, and they are distributed very unequally. If you grow up in a family of professionals, you inherit a certain amount of knowledge about the working world. You might know what different jobs involve, how to approach an employer, or whom to ask for an introduction. If nobody in your family or community has ever worked in a particular industry, or even worked at all, the professional world can feel intimidatingly opaque. An internship can help bridge that divide. It introduces you to people who can teach you things, vouch for your abilities, and open doors you might not even have known existed. In that sense, a first professional experience can be a vehicle for social mobility even if it doesn't immediately lead to a permanent job.

Another benefit, I hope, is that the process of independently sourcing a work experience could nurture a certain entrepreneurial spirit. One of the things I admire about Silicon Valley is the belief that _you can just do things_. You can email someone you've never met, or start a project without permission, or build something and convince other people to care about it. The world is surprisingly open to people who are willing to ask. Of course, that culture is enabled by an enormous amount of capital, privilege, and institutional support, but I think there is something valuable in the underlying mindset. Sometimes it takes someone going out, finding an opportunity, and succeeding to realise that they can actually just make things happen. I think that is something we should actively cultivate.

Of course, we would need guardrails against the many potential pitfalls with this proposal. There is also the much larger problem that South Africa simply does not have enough jobs and no amount of internships and funding will make that problem disappear. We need economic growth, capital investment, better infrastructure, and a business environment in which companies can expand and hire. A program like this one that I am proposing would be a complement to those reforms, not a substitute.

I should also acknowledge that there are already some brilliant initiatives working in this space. The Youth Employment Service (YES) is perhaps the most obvious example. It works with the private sector to provide young South Africans with twelve-month paid workplace experiences, helping them gain skills and a foothold in the labour market. There are also efforts like SA Youth and the broader Presidential Youth Employment Intervention, which help connect young people to earning and learning opportunities. It is clear that South Africa does not have a shortage of people who care about youth unemployment, nor is it short of programs trying to address it. 

I realise I've written quite a lot about what is, in essence, a fairly simple idea. I think it reflects a broader philosophy that I feel quite strongly about. There's something about dignity in agency. Most programs treat young people as recipients: you're assessed, sorted and placed. I propose to treat them as authors of their own lives. There's a difference between giving someone an opportunity and giving someone the means to create their own. I am betting that the latter doesn't just change what they can do but also that it might change what they believe they're capable of doing, which in turn, can transform entire countries.

Anyways, that's all from me (for now). If all this is still an unsolved problem in a few years' time, when I have the bandwidth to focus my efforts on such endeavours, please hold me to standing on this business.
